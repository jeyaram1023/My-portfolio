IEEE-Style Literature Survey Narrative

Recent advancements in smart key systems for Electric Vehicles (EVs) have predominantly leveraged wireless technologies to enhance user convenience. Studies on Radio Frequency Identification (RFID) and Near Field Communication (NFC) protocols [1], [3] highlight their lightweight nature, suitable for resource-constrained automotive IoT environments. However, these systems inherently suffer from static credential exposures, making them highly susceptible to cloning and skimming attacks. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed dynamic cryptographic handshakes; yet, the fundamental wireless vector remains exposed.

Similarly, the integration of Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) for Phone-as-a-Key (PaaK) systems has been extensively documented in recent literature [2], [5]. While offering superior cryptographic payload capabilities compared to standard RFID, BLE-based immobilizer architectures are notoriously vulnerable to Relay Attacks (e.g., utilizing signal amplifiers to bridge the distance between the key fob/phone and the vehicle). Works such as [4] attempt to solve this via time-of-flight (ToF) distance bounding, but such methods introduce significant processing overhead and hardware complexity.

In the context of hardware-level security, ESP32 and ESP8266 microcontrollers have emerged as robust platforms for embedded authentication, offering hardware acceleration for AES encryption [6]. Current literature, however, predominantly focuses on securing their wireless stacks (Wi-Fi/Bluetooth). A significant research gap exists in utilizing these powerful IoT microcontrollers exclusively through wired buses (UART/I2C/SPI) for automotive physical access. By stripping away the vulnerable wireless attack surface, a wired architecture intrinsically neutralizes relay and interception threats. Furthermore, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) for continuous authentication and behavioral anomaly detection at the edge remains underexplored in wired EV immobilizer contexts.

Therefore, our proposed system directly addresses these critical gaps. By architecting an ESP32-based wired communication framework, augmented with AES-128 dynamic passkeys, hardware unique ID binding, and AI-driven tamper detection logic, this work presents a resilient, multi-layer embedded security paradigm that fundamentally obsoletes standard wireless attack vectors.

Identified Research Gaps

  • Over-reliance on Wireless Vectors: Nearly all recent automotive access control research focuses on securing inherently flawed wireless protocols (BLE, UWB, NFC) rather than exploring secure physical/wired alternatives.
  • Lack of Edge AI in Hardware Keys: While ML is used in network intrusion detection, its application directly within the embedded microcontroller (ESP32) to detect physical tampering or anomalous connection behaviors is scarce.
  • Static Hardware Binding: Existing embedded systems often rely solely on static MAC addresses or UIDs, lacking dynamic, constantly rotating hardware-level passkeys linked to physical connection parameters.
  • Siloed Security Layers: Research tends to isolate cryptographic software security from hardware tamper detection. Integrated multi-layer approaches are needed.

Future Research Directions

  • TinyML Integration: Optimizing AI anomaly detection models to run natively on the ESP32 to monitor UART/I2C timing characteristics for intrusion attempts.
  • Physical Unclonable Functions (PUFs): Exploring how the silicon variances of the ESP32 can be used to generate the initial seed for the AES-128 encryption key.
  • Quantum-Resistant Wired Protocols: Future-proofing the wired communication channel using lightweight post-quantum cryptographic algorithms tailored for embedded devices.
  • Automotive Standard Compliance: Adapting the proposed UART/I2C logic to comply directly with automotive ISO 26262 functional safety and SAE J3061 cybersecurity standards.
